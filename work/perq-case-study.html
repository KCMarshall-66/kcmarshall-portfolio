PERQ Software  ·  2015–2019

Building PERQ's UX team, and the work that reached 1M+ consumers

I joined as the only UX person on a lead-engagement product. Four years later I was running a team of six that designed custom consumer experiences for apartment communities, car dealers, and furniture retailers.

Lead UX Architect, then UX Manager

1M+ consumers reached 6 designers and researchers hired 3 markets: multi-family, automotive, furniture

What PERQ sold

PERQ put interactive experiences on top of a customer's existing website to turn visitors into better leads. Think credit pre-approvals, shopping guides, buying quizzes, and reward offers. My first job was designing the template library those experiences were built from.

Shopping assistant template
Most visitors came in on their phones.

Customers didn't want a toolkit

The templates worked. They brought in buyers and turned them into stronger leads. But once we watched real customers use the platform, it was obvious they had no interest in setting up and running these experiences themselves. They wanted PERQ to build custom ones for their brand and their audience.

That changed the business. We went from selling self-serve templates to designing and running a custom experience for each client. The demand was far more than one designer could handle, and we had no design system, no research process, and no agreed way of working.

Building the team

So I went from being the only UX person to building a department. I hired and onboarded six designers and researchers who could match the quality bar and keep up with the volume.

Hiring was the easy part. Keeping good people meant giving them a reason to stay and a way to grow. I wrote career rubrics for junior, mid, and senior levels so everyone knew where they stood. I held regular one-on-ones, set up a review cycle with clear criteria, and started lunch-and-learns for the junior designers. As the team grew, I became the person who represented UX to PERQ's leadership.

How we worked

Six people designing custom work across three markets drifts apart fast without shared ground. I wrote up a research and design process the whole team followed, built a component library and design system so experiences stayed consistent, and documented handoff standards so new hires could ramp up quickly.

We also moved onto the same agile rhythm as engineering, with standups, pointing, grooming, planning, reviews, and retros. That gave design a predictable cadence and a regular place to fix what wasn't working.

The work

Every market needed something different. Car buyers needed credit pre-qualification and shopping help tied to real inventory. Apartment hunters needed floor plan explorers and ways to compare communities. Furniture shoppers responded best to style quizzes.

Mobile reviews and motion studies

As more traffic moved to phones, we ran regular mobile reviews that looked at touch targets, legibility, and where flows got stuck on small screens. For multi-step flows like floor plans and quizzes, static mockups didn't explain enough, so we made motion studies to settle the interaction before it went to engineering.

Looking back

PERQ is where I went from designer to manager. The surprise was how much of the job was still design. I had to figure out what people needed to do good work and then take away whatever was getting in their way.

The design system, the process doc, and the lunch-and-learns weren't side projects. They're the reason six people could work like one team and get experiences in front of over a million consumers without the quality slipping.
