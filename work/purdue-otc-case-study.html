Purdue Research Foundation · 2023

They asked for a new homepage. The problem was everywhere else.

Purdue's Office of Technology Commercialization licenses university inventions to industry. Research showed that the people most likely to license them, technology scouts and investors, couldn't find anything on the site.

UX research lead. Stakeholder sessions, interviews, journey map, service blueprint, IA, wireframes, and an Axure prototype.

Purdue OTC runs one of the largest technology transfer programs at any US research university. It protects Purdue's intellectual property and licenses patented work that's now used in more than 100 countries. They came to us for a homepage redesign.

We started with stakeholder interviews, user research, and an audit of the existing site. It quickly became clear that a new homepage would just be a fresh coat of paint over a structural problem.

Homepage concept from the redesign.

What was actually wrong

  • The categories were OTC's, not the industry's. Inventions were filed under internal labels instead of the standard classifications scouts and investors use. You couldn't browse in any way that made sense to them.
  • Search was the only way in. There was no browsable catalog, and search results were clumsy. A scout had to know exactly what they wanted before they could find it, which is the opposite of scouting.
  • The documents didn't work. The technology PDFs had vague titles and no links, and they didn't follow the format scouts need to catalog an invention and pitch it to an investor.
  • The site mirrored the org chart. Seven top-level nav items, most with deep dropdowns. Important pages were three or four levels down. A scout and a professor submitting a disclosure got exactly the same site.

So the project grew from a homepage update into rethinking how Purdue packages its inventions and gets them in front of the right people.

Who we talked to

The site has three outside audiences. Purdue researchers want to disclose and commercialize their work. Technology scouts find inventions to recommend to the companies they represent. Investors and licensees want to fund or license Purdue technology. Behind all of them is the OTC and PRF staff who keep the site running.

I ran whiteboard sessions with stakeholders on goals, how success is measured, and who actually visits the site. I walked through the site and the internal workflows from both the inventor's side and the licensee's. Then I interviewed people in each group about how they find university technology today, step by step, and what words they use for it. Matching their vocabulary turned out to matter a lot.

Making sense of it

The scout became the primary outside user, and I built an empathy map around them. It covered what they see when comparing different universities' sites, what they think inside an innovation database, and what they do when they can't find something fast.

A two-track journey map followed inventors and licensees from first awareness through follow-up. Both groups got frustrated at the same stage, browsing, because there was no guidance and not enough depth. The service blueprint showed everything behind the site: WordPress, HubSpot, and Absorb LMS on the front end, and Google Drive and Salesforce behind the staff. I also wrote two inventor personas, one for a first-time discloser and one for a researcher already managing active IP.

The redesign

The biggest structural change was splitting the homepage by role. Investors and scouts get trending technologies, category snapshots, and the path to licensing. Inventors get a clear route to disclosure forms, inventor resources, and the Purdue Inventor Hub. Nobody has to wade through the other group's content.

For scouts, I added a category grid with live counts, like Electrical Engineering (243) and Mechanical Engineering (207), so they get a sense of the portfolio the moment they arrive. Quick search got category, subcategory, and date filters that match how scouts actually search. I also added newsletter signup by category, so a scout only gets alerts for the fields they cover.

The listing page switched to industry-standard categories. It added filters for people, keywords, and categories, showed the lead inventor, and let scouts bookmark technologies to build a shortlist for their investors. A licensing module and a short credentials block sit right under the browse section: #6 in the world for patents, 400+ technologies available, used in 100+ countries.

Visually, we kept it dark and confident, using Purdue gold only for actions and key numbers. Scouts are comparing Purdue to every other tech transfer office, so it needed to look like it belonged with the best of them.

Testing it

The Axure prototype covered the homepage, browsing, the inventor disclosure path, and the licensing path. In testing, scouts didn't run into the navigation confusion they'd described in interviews. They found technologies faster with the new categories, and they told us the trending module was the kind of quick scan that would bring them back regularly.

Open the Axure prototype Innovation cards with the new taxonomy, TRL maturity, inventor attribution, and a one-field newsletter signup.

Looking back

Reframing a client's request takes trust. They asked for one thing, and we had to show enough rigor early on to earn the right to tell them it was something bigger. Most of the research was really about that, getting everyone to the same understanding so a bigger fix felt reasonable rather than like scope creep.

The look and feel came from the interviews too. When scouts described the tech transfer sites they trust and keep going back to, they kept saying the same few things, and we designed to those.
