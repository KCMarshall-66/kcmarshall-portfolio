The brief: design a vendor risk platform for enterprise security teams

The prompt was an AI-assisted product that helps large companies assess and monitor the risk their vendors bring in. The category is Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM). It's mature and expensive, and most people who work in it will tell you it's broken. Programs still run on annual questionnaires, shared spreadsheets, and email, so a team only really knows a vendor's status on the day of the review.

I held off on screens until I understood the people doing the work, the competitors, and the regulations those people answer to. That took longer than drawing a dashboard, but nearly every decision later on came out of it.

The failure I kept coming back to: a vendor passes its annual review, gets breached five months later, and nobody on the risk team finds out until it's already a problem.

Grounding the design in real people and real compliance stakes

Compliance drives almost everything in TPRM. A risk team at a global bank might be answering to DORA in the EU, OSFI B-10 and B-13 in Canada, NYDFS in New York, and the EU AI Act all at once. Their frustrations and the calls they have to make come straight out of those rules. I wrote three personas covering the manager, a senior analyst, and a junior analyst.

A design system built from the brand identity outward

I set up the identity and design system before the screens. A tessera is one tile in a mosaic, so the mark is a geometric cube. I pulled the blues from its three faces: the dark one for headers and nav, the mid one for interactive elements, and the light one for charts. Type is IBM Plex Sans in three weights.

I checked every color token against WCAG 2.1 AA on white, on the canvas color, and on each status tint. Badges and rows always pair color with a label or icon. People who buy compliance software will notice if that's missing.

The dashboard: four questions Dana answers every morning

Dana starts each day by going through whatever came in overnight. I built the dashboard around the four questions she asks herself. What needs action now? What is the team working on? Is work coming in faster than it's getting closed? Where is the risk concentrated?

I put the risk score right next to the arrivals-vs-closed tile on purpose. When the backlog grows, the score goes up, and with the two side by side Dana can see that without having to work it out.

Dana's dashboard. The four tiles across the top, then her investigation tasks ordered by how much each one adds to the risk score.

"Waiting on vendor" and "waiting on our team" are split out. If her team is the bottleneck, Dana moves work around. If the vendor is, she goes to someone more senior on their side. A single average age would hide which one it is.

Five interconnected screens covering the full risk lifecycle

The prototype walks through intake, monitoring, evidence review, assessments, and data mapping. Each screen is built for whoever uses it most, but they all share the same shell, nav, and status system, so moving between them doesn't feel like switching apps.

The vendor register: 612 vendors you can filter by tier, region, owner, and status. Tiers are set automatically using DORA, OSFI, and US interagency criteria.
Security reviews, RoPA entries, DPIAs, and EU AI Act screenings all land in one queue. Priya and Marcus each filter down to their own work.
Monitoring scans 4,318 public sources, including breach dumps, code repos, CVE feeds, certificate logs, and news and filings. The AI matches each signal to a vendor in Northbridge's portfolio.

Three design decisions that shaped the product

These are the three places where I could have reasonably gone another way, and the reasoning behind where I landed.

Monitoring is the main feature
Every competitor I looked at sells monitoring as an add-on to the assessment workflow. I flipped that. The main thing on Dana's dashboard is a task list driven by monitoring signals. Assessments still happen, but most of the value comes from what Tessera catches between them.
A person signs off on every AI finding
Every AI finding shows a confidence score and a source, and someone has to review it before it changes a vendor's risk rating. The EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF both require that for high-risk uses. For buyers in this space, being able to show that is a selling point too.
The team score shows its parts
The team score breaks out into its four inputs: closed on time, time to verify, vendor follow-ups, and reopen rate. Marcus worries that SLA misses make him look bad, and a plain leaderboard would make him distrust the tool. Priya's escalations would look like slow work without the context of what's in her queue. I meant it to help Dana coach people.

Reflection

What surprised me was how much the regulations shaped the UX. Dana has to satisfy three regulators, and each one defines "critical vendor" a little differently. How do you give her one system of record when what that record has to contain changes between Toronto, London, and New York?

Where I landed was one register with a separate view for each framework, plus AI that flags possible EU AI Act issues early so they don't turn into last-minute calls to Legal. If I did a second round, I'd test those framework views with real risk analysts first.
