Redesigning Schlage.com

Schlage makes locks and door hardware. They have a huge catalog, a broad customer base, and a website that hadn't kept up with how people shop. I led UX on the rebuild and on the design system underneath it, which we ran at the same time.

UX Director at The Basement. Research, IA, design system, prototyping, and testing.

The find-a-finish prototype, one of the flows we tested in motion before it went to engineering.

Starting with people

I began with a heuristic review of the old site and interviews with Schlage's stakeholders, so I understood what the business needed before talking to customers. Then I interviewed end users, ran usability tests on the existing flows, and audited the tech stack so we'd know the platform's limits before we designed around them.

That gave us two primary personas, Charles and Amy, plus a journey map and a service blueprint. With those, everyone on the project was designing for the same people, and the team stopped working from assumptions about what customers wanted.

The design system came first

I set up the design system before designing any pages. It followed atomic design: color and type as atoms, buttons and icon pairs as molecules, headers, footers, and feature blocks as organisms, then templates. We built it as an Adobe XD component library, so a change to an atom showed up on every artboard the team had.

It slowed the first few weeks. After that, every new page was built from parts that were already specified and documented, and the developers always had a clear spec to work from.

Prototyping the tricky parts

You can't really test favoriting, search, or inventory-driven layouts from static artboards. We built motion prototypes for those interactions and used them for both testing and dev handoff. For mobile nav, we prototyped several approaches and tested them against each other before choosing one.

We also documented how each element should behave when the inventory data changed. That doc collected stakeholder feedback and kept development aligned.

Testing as we went

It's easy to look at a design and say "I like it." I wanted numbers, so testing happened throughout the project, with a different method for each question.

  • Tree testing in UXTweak before we built any navigation. It showed where people expected to find things and where our proposed IA confused them.
  • Prototype testing in Maze with high-fidelity Axure prototypes. Completion rates, misclicks, and path data showed friction our own reviews had missed.
  • Moderated interviews, synthesized in Mural, to explain the why behind the numbers and decide which problems hurt conversion most.

Looking back

Running the design system and the site redesign together was hard, and I'd do it the same way again. Teams often treat the system as cleanup to do once the pages settle, and that guarantees rework. Building it first meant everything after it was already reusable and documented, and it paid for itself by the first big feature.

I'd keep the testing rhythm too. Every round turned up at least one problem that would have cost a lot more to fix after launch. You don't have to test everything. Test the decisions that would be most expensive to get wrong.
