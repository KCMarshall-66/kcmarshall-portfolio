DMI Consulting · 2019

Cutting call times in half at a health insurer's call centers

50%drop in average call handling time across Medicare, Medicaid, and private lines

Sr. UX Architect at DMI, working for Optum (UnitedHealthcare) on behalf of Triple-S Salud. Site visits, workshops, interviews, personas, journey maps, and service blueprints.

The setup

Optum brought DMI in to help Triple-S Salud, a large health insurer in Puerto Rico. Their call centers took calls from Medicare, Medicaid, and private plan members, and also from doctors, providers, and pharmacists. Every type of caller needed different processes, different systems, and different knowledge to get resolved.

The reps weren't the problem. Each insurance line had built its own tools, workflows, and coding standards over the years, and nobody had ever looked at the whole thing together. The reps had quietly learned to work around it.

Sitting with the reps

You can't understand a call center from a conference room, so I went to the floor. I watched reps jump between systems, look things up mid-call, and juggle complicated coverage questions with someone waiting on the line. Watching showed me things interviews wouldn't have. I saw the little pauses, the workarounds, and the spots where people slowed down because the tool wasn't helping.

I also ran workshops with stakeholders and subject matter experts from all three lines, alongside DMI's business process consultants. Then I did one-on-one interviews with reps (SCRs and CSRs). I'd start broad and work toward specific calls, so they could describe the problems in their own words.

Two personas

I wrote two personas from the visits, interviews, and workshops. One was the SCR, who takes the incoming calls. The other was the CSR, who handles the operations side of resolving a case. The biggest thing they did was change how meetings went. People stopped saying "I think users want..." and started saying "the SCR needs..."

The blueprints are where it clicked

Journey maps showed how a call felt from the rep's side and where frustration piled up. The service blueprints went further. Once I'd mapped the front-stage and back-stage process for each insurance line, the inconsistencies were impossible to miss. Each blueprint also showed exactly where reps had invented their own fixes to cover the gaps.

What we found

Medicare, Medicaid, and private had each been built on their own. A rep who handled all three wasn't looking at three views of one system. They were switching between three different jobs, sometimes in the middle of a single call.

  • Three systems that didn't match. Different platforms, layouts, and interactions. Every time a call crossed lines, the rep had to switch tools.
  • Codes that didn't match. The same procedure or coverage case could have a different code depending on the line. Reps were translating codes in their heads, often at the most sensitive moment of the call.
  • Homemade workarounds. Sticky notes, personal cheat sheets, and shorthand that wasn't written down anywhere official. Everyone's were different, which made onboarding hard and meant a lot of knowledge left when people did.
  • No documentation across lines. Each line kept its own docs. Nothing covered a call that touched more than one, which is exactly where reps needed it most.

What changed

We made three recommendations. Standardize UI and process patterns across the lines. Normalize the coding so reps didn't have to translate. And take the best of the reps' own workarounds and make them official process. The blueprints became the document that ops leadership, the process consultants, and the tech teams all worked from.

Average call handling time dropped by half.

Looking back

The best thing I did on this project was get out of the meeting room. Workshops show you how an organization sees its own problems, and that isn't always the same as the problems themselves. I saw a rep stop mid-call to check a reference sheet they'd made themselves, and that taught me more than any process doc.

I'd do the workaround audit again in a heartbeat. People usually treat workarounds as non-standard behavior to clean up. Here, every one pointed straight at a gap in the official process. That's why the recommendations were specific enough that people believed them.
